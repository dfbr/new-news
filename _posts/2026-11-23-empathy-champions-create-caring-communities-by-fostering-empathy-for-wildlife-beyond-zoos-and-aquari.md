  beyond zoos and aquariums

Posted by Gigi Allianic, CommunicationsThrough a Woodland Park Zoo-led initiative, zoo and aquarium professionals are bringing wildlife conservation into communities across the United States, inspiring people to turn care for animals into action.&nbsp; Empathy Champions are part of a leadership development program developed by the zoo-staffed Advancing Conservation through Empathy for Wildlife® (ACE for Wildlife®) Network that focuses on conservation-minded community outreach. Champions bring research-informed public engagement practices outside zoo and aquarium walls and into collaborations with partners as diverse as healthcare clinics, tourism companies, and state government agencies. By teaming up, Empathy Champions and peer organizations help public audiences consider what caring and conservation actions in their own lives can help animals and people thrive.Lake Superior Zoo in Duluth, Minn. and First Street Pediatrics created a space for young patients and families to read board books (pictured) about local zoo animals facing their own health challenges. Photo courtesy of Lake Superior Zoo.In the program’s first year, professionals from zoos and aquariums across nine different states co-designed inspiring public engagement activities with their partners. In Duluth, Minn., Lake Superior Zoo and First Street Pediatrics created a space for young patients and families to read board books about local zoo animals facing their own health challenges and then write “get well soon” cards to the animals. In Seward, Alaska, a wildlife tour operator worked with Alaska SeaLife Center interpreters and Alaska Native communities to infuse interpretive practices that foster empathy for wildlife into captain and crew engagements with tourists during wildlife observation cruises. A full catalog of Empathy Champions activities is available to explore on the Network’s website.“It’s inspiring to see our Network bringing empathy for wildlife into communities in new and meaningful ways,” said Dr. Marta Burnet, Director of Advancing Empathy at Woodland Park Zoo. “Empathy Champions are building on a decade of learning in zoos and aquariums to help more people see themselves as conservationists—and turn care for animals and people into action.”An Aldabra tortoise is hand-fed by an animal keeper at Woodland Park Zoo's Wild Encounters. Photo by Jeremy Dwyer-Lindgren/Woodland Park ZooThe first-year projects are also generating practical lessons for conservation outreach more broadly. They show that empathy can create common ground with unconventional partners, like a regional health care system, and that effective collaborations work best with multiple perspectives—from animal care, science, and interpretation to healthcare, tourism, and Indigenous knowledge. Hands-on mentoring in real-world settings helped partners adopt new practices, and strong public interest demonstrated that conservation-focused experiences can support both community engagement and partner goals. A new cohort of Empathy Champions begins their work this month, leveraging their previous experiences developing empathy initiatives to design outreach projects in rural, suburban and urban communities throughout the country. This year’s Empathy Champions hail from Dakota Zoo (Bismarck, N.D.), Detroit Zoological Society (Royal Oak, Mich.), Fresno Chaffee Zoo (Fresno, Calif.), Minnesota Zoo (Apple Valley, Minn.), the Peggy Notebaert Nature Museum of the Chicago Academy of Sciences (Chicago), Point Defiance Zoo &amp; Aquarium (Tacoma, Wash.), Woodland Park Zoo (Seattle), and the Zoological Society of Milwaukee (Milwaukee).Empathy brainstorm! Photo by Jeremy Dwyer-Lindgren/Woodland Park ZooProfessionals who are interested in learning more about empathy’s connection to wildlife conservation are invited to explore the Network’s online introduction to empathy at introduction.aceforwildlife.org. About the Advancing Conservation through Empathy (ACE for Wildlife®) Network In 2019, Woodland Park Zoo founded the ACE for Wildlife Network to facilitate the shaolring of knowledge, experiences, and data to drive conservation change through fostering empathy for animals and the environment that sustains them. Today, the Network is a global community of over a thousand educators, animal keepers, communicators, and leaders who are using empathy practices to advance conservation action. Professionals in the Network benefit from support and training for their professional growth and can influence their field through the innovative work that they are piloting. The ACE for Wildlife Network is supported through grant funding from Margaret A. Cargill Philanthropies. Learn more at the Network website, aceforwildlife.org, or by emailing empathy@zoo.org.
